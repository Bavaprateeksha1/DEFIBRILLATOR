Procedure checklist

    Equipment and theory

    Defibrillator, ECG simulator, DSO, discharge pads (2).

    Why defibrillate?

    In ventricular fibrillation the heart muscle contracts without coordination, so cardiac output stops and brain damage follows within about 5 minutes. A high-energy shock makes all fibres contract at once so the heart’s own pacemaker can take over again.

    How the circuit works

    A variable autotransformer feeds a high-voltage transformer and rectifier. A change-over switch first charges a capacitor (position 1). Pressing discharge moves the switch to position 2 and the capacitor empties through an inductor and the patient. The inductor shapes the pulse, but its resistance wastes some energy. The chest load is roughly 50–100 Ω.

    Synch and manual modes

    Synch mode waits for the R-wave of the QRS complex so the shock never lands on the T-wave. Use it for normal rhythm and A-fib. V-fib has no QRS, so it needs manual mode.

    Monophasic vs biphasic

    Monophasic pulses run 3–12 ms. Biphasic pulses reverse polarity and studies suggest they defibrillate more effectively, so they need less energy.

    More theory

    Atrial vs ventricular fibrillation

    In atrial fibrillation the atria quiver and the ventricles respond irregularly, so the QRS complexes are still present but the rhythm is irregular and the P-wave is lost. The patient usually has a pulse, which is why a synchronised shock (cardioversion) is used. In ventricular fibrillation the ventricles only quiver, there is no pulse and no QRS, so an immediate unsynchronised shock is needed.

    Energy stored in the capacitor

    The stored energy is E = ½CV². For example, a 32 µF capacitor charged to about 4.7 kV stores roughly 350 J. The energy actually delivered to the patient is lower, because some is lost in the inductor’s resistance and in the electrode and skin interface.

    Why the R-wave is used for synchronisation

    The T-wave is the vulnerable period of the cardiac cycle. A shock that lands there can push the heart into ventricular fibrillation (the R-on-T effect). By triggering the discharge from the R-wave, the defibrillator places the shock safely away from the T-wave. You can see this on the simulator: a manual shock on a normal rhythm or A-fib may induce V-fib.

    External and internal defibrillation

    External paddles are placed on the chest wall, so more energy is needed because the current passes through skin, fat, ribs and lung. Internal paddles are held directly on the heart during open-chest surgery and need far less energy. Conductive gel or pads reduce the contact resistance and prevent skin burns.

    Paddle placement

    In the standard position one paddle goes below the right collarbone beside the sternum and the other on the left side of the chest over the apex of the heart, so that the current passes through the heart muscle.

    Safety precautions

    Nobody should touch the patient or the bed during discharge, so the operator announces “clear” before pressing the buttons. Remove flammable materials and any oxygen flow near the chest, and never discharge the paddles in the air or against each other. After the shock, check the rhythm and the pulse.

    Manual defibrillators and AEDs

    A manual defibrillator lets a trained operator read the rhythm, choose the mode and set the energy. An automated external defibrillator (AED) analyses the rhythm itself and only advises a shock when it detects a shockable rhythm such as V-fib, which makes it suitable for the public. Defibrillation does not help in asystole (a flat line).

    Typical energy levels

    Monophasic devices commonly start at 200 to 360 J for V-fib. Biphasic devices achieve similar success at lower energy, often 120 to 200 J. Cardioversion for A-fib generally uses lower settings, starting around 100 J, which is the reasoning behind the thresholds in this simulator.
