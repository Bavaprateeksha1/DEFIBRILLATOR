Theory

Ventricular fibrillation is a serious cardiac emergency resulting from asynchronous contraction of the heart muscle. This uncoordinated movement of the ventricle walls may result from electric shock or abnormalities of body chemistry. The main problem is continuous stimulation of adjacent cells of the heart muscle fibres, so there is no synchronized succession of events following heart action. Fibrillation leads to loss of cardiac output and irreversible brain damage or death if not reversed within about 5 minutes of onset.

Ventricular fibrillation can be converted into a more efficient rhythm by applying a high-energy shock to the heart. This sudden surge makes all muscle fibres contract simultaneously, after which the fibres may respond to the normal physiological intrinsic pulse. The horizontal line after the shock on the cardiograph shows that the recorder was blocked or disconnected for its protection during the shock.

Defibrillator

The instrument for administering the shock is called a defibrillator; it is used to reverse fibrillation and re-establish normal activity. The shock can be delivered through electrodes placed on the patient's chest (external defibrillation) or held directly against the heart when the chest is open (internal defibrillation). Higher voltage is required for external than internal defibrillation.

Working Principle

A variable auto-transformer forms the primary of a high-voltage transformer. Its output is rectified by a diode rectifier and connected to a vacuum-type high-voltage change-over switch. In position 1 the capacitor (oil-filled, microfarad range) charges to a voltage set by the auto-transformer. When the shock is delivered (foot switch or push-button on the electrode handle), the switch changes to position 2 and the capacitor discharges across the heart through the electrodes.

An inductor slows the discharge through induced counter-voltage, giving a physiologically favourable pulse shape; its drawback is its own resistance, which dissipates part of the energy. The waveform depends on the capacitor and inductor values. The patient is approximately a 50-100 Ω resistance for 80 cm² electrodes. Monophasic pulse width is typically programmable 3-12 ms; biphasic positive 3-10 ms and negative 1-10 ms. Biphasic pulses appear more effective than monophasic ones.
